10 Fatal Screenplay Formatting Mistakes That Make Producers Pass

Studio script readers read hundreds of submissions every month looking for any excuse to stop reading and assign a "PASS." Discover the ten technical red flags that instantly mark your script as amateur before page five.

BY DRAFTSRUSH STUDIO TOPIC: SCRIPT COVERAGE RED FLAGS 12 MIN READ
TL;DR // The Fatal 10 Red Flags

Quick Diagnostic Checklist

  • 1. The "Brick of Text": Action paragraphs thicker than 4 lines that choke white space.
  • 2. Directing on the Page: Commanding camera angles (CLOSE UP, PAN TO) instead of describing focal objects.
  • 3. Unfilmable Novelistic Prose: Explaining internal feelings and backstories that cannot be photographed.
  • 4. Parenthetical Abuse (Wrylies): Overusing performance directions before dialogue lines.
  • 5. Broken Scene Headings: Omitting INT./EXT. designations or writing vague times of day.
  • 6. On-The-Nose Dialogue: Characters explicitly stating inner motives without conversational subtext.
  • 7. The Passive Voice Trap: Using "is running" or "begins to cry" instead of active present-tense verbs.
  • 8. Page Count Bloat: Submitting 125+ page spec scripts that scream an undisciplined second act.
  • 9. Non-Standard Typography: Using Arial, Times New Roman, or non-Courier 12pt fonts.
  • 10. Orphaned Elements & Bad Pagination: Leaving character cues or transitions stranded alone at the bottom of pages.
Industry Reality

The 10-Page Rule of Hollywood Coverage

When a literary manager, agency assistant, or studio reader opens your PDF, they are not looking for reasons to love it—they are looking for a reason to assign a quick PASS and move to the next script on their stack.

Formatting errors tell a professional reader two things: first, that you have not invested time studying the craft; second, that your script will be an expensive, undisciplined headache for the production crew.

Detailed Audit

All 10 Mistakes Deconstructed

Error 01

The "Brick of Text" Action Block

Nothing fatigues a coverage reader faster than a nine-line paragraph describing a room's furniture. In screenwriting, white space is king. Action lines must be broken into punchy bursts of 2 to 4 lines that mimic camera cuts and direct the reader's eye rapidly down the page.

Error 02

Directing on the Page (Camera Cues)

Writing THE CAMERA PANS OVER TO REVEAL or CLOSE UP ON JANE'S EYE alienates directors. Guide the reader's visual focus through description alone. If you describe the glint of a knife on the table on its own line, the director already knows it's an insert shot without you commanding it.

Error 03

Unfilmable Novelistic Prose

Writing "Marcus wonders if he should have stayed in Chicago, remembering his mother's deathbed promise" cannot be photographed. Unless an actor speaks it or an action visualizes it, internal monologues are invisible. Write only what can be seen through a camera lens or heard through a microphone.

Error 04

Parenthetical Over-Use (Wrylies)

Putting parenthetical cues like (angrily), (sarcastically), or (with deep remorse) before every single spoken line insults the actors' intelligence. Use parentheticals only when a line's delivery is the exact opposite of what the words say.

Error 05

On-The-Nose Dialogue

When characters say exactly what they are thinking ("I am betraying you because I resented your promotion"), subtext dies. Real people use deflection, humor, lies, and silence to conceal vulnerability. Trim dialogue bloat using our free dialogue word pruner.

Error 06

Broken & Vague Scene Headings (Sluglines)

A slugline has one technical job: tell the production manager whether to rent lighting (DAY or NIGHT) and where the crew is standing (INT. or EXT.). Avoid writing poetic times like LUSCIOUS SUNSET or MOMENTS LATER in place of standard time markers.

Error 07

The Passive Voice & "Ing" Trap

Screenplays happen in immediate real-time. Avoid passive progressive verbs: don't write "Frank is running toward the door, gasping for breath and beginning to panic." Write: "Frank runs for the door. He gasps. Panics." Active present verbs instantly accelerate the reader's pulse.

Error 08

Page Count Bloat (The 120+ Page Spec)

Established auteur directors can shoot a 160-page script. An unproduced writer submitting a 135-page spec script triggers instant alarm bells. It immediately signals that the writer doesn't know how to edit or prune secondary subplots. Keep first feature specs between 90 and 105 pages.

Error 09

Non-Courier Typography & Margin Meddling

Never format a screenplay in Helvetica, Arial, or Times New Roman. Courier 12pt is a fixed-width monospaced font where every character takes identical space, establishing the 1-page-equals-1-minute rule. Altering margins or font size to artificially hit page targets is spotted by experienced readers in seconds.

Error 10

Orphaned Elements & Careless Pagination

Leaving a character's name at the very bottom of a page with their dialogue pushed to the next page is an automatic amateur marker. Professional screenwriting software automatically handles "orphan protection" and breaks dialogue blocks with (MORE) and (CONT'D) tags.

Common Search Queries

Frequently Asked Questions: Formatting Rejections

Is it acceptable to write spec scripts in Word or Google Docs?

Only if you use a strict pre-formatted template. Word processors default to variable-width fonts and incorrect tabs. It is safer to write directly inside dedicated software like our free online screenplay editor to ensure exact 12pt Courier margin geometry.

How long is too long for a spec screenplay?

Any first-time spec script over 115 pages faces immediate scrutiny. 90 to 105 pages is the modern industry sweet spot.
