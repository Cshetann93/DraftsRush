Dialogue Craft & Voice

Top 5 Exercises to Write Realistic, Non-Cringe Dialogue

Bad dialogue happens when characters say exactly what they mean, answer questions directly, and explain the plot to each other. These five practical writing exercises force subtext, tension, and natural rhythm onto the page.

By Lead Writer: CHETAN • UPDATED FOR 2026 • 8 MIN READ
TL;DR

5 Practical Dialogue Workouts

If your characters sound like textbooks or clones of the writer, run your scene through these drills:

  • The Subtext Strip Drill: Ban characters from answering questions directly; force them to deflect, attack, or negotiate.
  • The 12-Word Sentence Cap: Limit every single line of dialogue to 12 words or fewer to match real human breath patterns.
  • Conflicting Secret Agendas: Give each speaker a private, unstated goal before they open their mouths.
  • The Name Blacklist: Ban characters from speaking each other's names to eliminate soap-opera cadence.
  • The Audio Playback Stress Test: Read lines into a voice memo or text-to-speech engine to catch clunky phrasing your eyes skim over.

Real people rarely speak in grammatically complete sentences. They talk around what they want, use diversion tactics, change the subject when embarrassed, and withhold vital information.

Amateur dialogue fails because it is too efficient. Character A asks a question, Character B answers honestly, and both characters speak with the same vocabulary, rhythm, and sentence length. The scene feels mechanical because there is no friction between what is thought and what is spoken.

Dialogue is not a tool to deliver exposition to the audience. Dialogue is verbal combat where characters maneuver to get what they want. Here are five practical drills you can use today to rewrite wooden lines into dynamic exchanges.

Exercise 01

The Subtext Strip Drill

DEFLECTION OVER INFORMATION

In everyday life, when people are challenged, they rarely give straightforward answers. They counter-attack, change the subject, focus on an irrelevant physical detail, or answer with another question.

ON-THE-NOSE (WEAK)

MARK
Did you take the money out of the safe?

SARAH
Yes, I took it because you haven't paid the mortgage and we are going to lose this house.

WITH SUBTEXT (STRONG)

MARK
Did you open the safe?

SARAH
The bank called twice this morning. The sheriff's notice is on the fridge.

MARK
That was our reserve fund.

SARAH
It was paper. Now we have a roof until Friday.

How to Run the Drill

Before writing a single line of dialogue, write two index cards. On Card A, write what Character A wants that they refuse to say out loud. On Card B, write what Character B wants. Now write them talking about something mundane, like ordering coffee or fixing a car. The tension hums beneath every syllable. Exercise 02

The 12-Word Sentence Cap

CADENCE & BREATH CONTROL

When writers sit at a keyboard, they write essay-style prose. When actors stand in front of a camera or creators speak into a microphone, sentences longer than 12 to 14 words sound formal, breathless, and stiff.

The Hard Limit: Maximum 12 words per line of spoken dialogue
The Goal: Force characters to volley words like tennis players
The Exception: Only 1 monologue allowed per act, earned by climax

Why It Works

Short sentences create room for pauses, interruptions, and eye contact. Short lines mimic natural speech rhythms where people search for words, change gears, and hand conversational control back and forth.

Exercise 03

Conflicting Secret Agendas

MOTIVE FRICTION

Boring scenes occur when both characters want the exact same thing (e.g., two detectives who agree the suspect is guilty and review facts together). Great dialogue requires underlying friction even in friendly exchanges.

CHARACTER A SECRET MOTIVE: Needs to borrow $5,000 without looking desperate or admitting financial collapse.
CHARACTER B SECRET MOTIVE: Needs Character A to work this weekend, but suspects Character A is secretly job hunting.

How to Run the Drill

Before writing a single line of dialogue, write two index cards. On Card A, write what Character A wants that they refuse to say out loud. On Card B, write what Character B wants. Now write them talking about something mundane, like ordering coffee or fixing a car. The tension hums beneath every syllable.

Exercise 04

The Name Blacklist

SOAP-OPERA CURE

In real life, people almost never use the other person's name during a conversation. You know who you are talking to; they know who you are talking to. In amateur scripts, characters address each other by name in every third sentence.

SOAP OPERA (AWKWARD)

"Listen to me, Jason. You have to stop doing this, Jason. I love you, Jason, but this is dangerous."

CLEAN CUT (NATURAL)

"Listen to me. You have to stop doing this. I love you. But this is dangerous."

How to Run the Drill

Run a search in your script for every character's name. Delete 95% of them from spoken dialogue. Save names strictly for moments of high emotional intervention, when getting someone's attention in a crowded room, or during formal introductions.

Exercise 05

The Audio Playback Stress Test

REAL-TIME DIAGNOSTIC

The human brain compensates when reading silently on a screen. You know what you intended the line to mean, so your internal voice reads it with perfect emotion and cadence. An actor or viewer hears only the literal words.

Method A (Text-to-Speech): Paste your scene into a neutral screen reader or audio generator. If the line sounds awkward when read by a monotone computer voice, it is structurally clunky.
Method B (The Voice Memo): Record yourself reading both parts into your phone. Listen back while doing the dishes or walking outside. Any line that makes you cringe or lose attention must be cut.

Why It Works

Your ears catch rhythmic stumbling blocks that your eyes will forgive every time. If your tongue trips over a syllable transition while reading aloud, an actor will trip over it on set.

The Golden Rule: Less Talking, More Action

Ninety percent of dialogue problems can be solved by cutting the lines completely and replacing them with physical behavior. Film and video are visual mediums. To trim wordy drafts automatically, test your script inside our free browser dialogue word pruner.

Instead of: "I am so anxious about this meeting."
Show: He shreds a paper napkin under the table into confetti.
Instead of: "I don't trust you anymore."
Show: She slides her car keys off the kitchen counter into her pocket.
