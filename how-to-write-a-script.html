First things first

You don't need a script idea. You need a story idea.

Maybe you have a character in mind. Maybe you can picture one scene. Maybe all you've got is a question that starts with “What if…?” That's enough to start.

Don't worry about scene headings, dialogue formatting, or how many pages a script should be. Those things can come later. First, let's figure out what you want to tell people.

One idea, step by step

Let's build a story together.

We'll use one simple example throughout. You can follow the same steps with your own idea.

Our starting idea

A woman receives a phone call from her own number. The call is coming from tomorrow.

01

Start with what happens.

Write your idea in one or two sentences. Don't try to make it perfect. Just explain the interesting thing that happens.

Our example

A woman gets a call from her own phone number. The voice on the other end sounds exactly like her, and it warns her not to leave the house tomorrow.

02

Decide who it's about.

Your main character is the person we follow. We need to know what matters to them, what they want, and what they're afraid of losing.

Ask yourself

  • Who is this person?
  • What do they want right now?
  • What makes this problem personal?
03

Give them a problem to solve.

A story starts moving when something gets in the character's way. The problem might be a secret, a difficult choice, another person, or something they can't explain.

In our story

She doesn't know whether to trust the warning. If she stays home, she might avoid danger—or miss the one thing she needs to discover why the call happened.

04

Work out a beginning, middle, and end.

You don't need a complicated formula. Think about three things: how the problem begins, how it gets harder, and what finally happens because of it.

BEGINNING

The strange call arrives.

MIDDLE

She investigates and finds details only her future self could know.

END

She discovers what the warning was really trying to prevent.

05

Turn the story into scenes.

A scene is one part of the story that happens in a particular place and time. Ask: what can the audience actually see or hear here? If nothing changes, you may not need the scene.

Possible scenes

  1. She is alone in her kitchen when the phone rings.
  2. She calls her own number back and hears a message she hasn't recorded yet.
  3. She finds a clue that connects the warning to tomorrow morning.
06

Let your characters speak.

Dialogue is what characters say out loud. It doesn't need to explain everything. Let people hesitate, avoid questions, misunderstand each other, or say less than they mean.

Read the lines aloud. If they don't sound like something a real person would say, rewrite them in your own words.

Now, see the difference

From an idea to a script page.

A screenplay describes what the audience sees and hears. Here's how the phone-call idea might look when written as a scene.

Sample screenplaySCENE 01

INT. KITCHEN — NIGHT

MAYA, 30s, stands alone in a dim kitchen. Her phone lights up on the table.

The caller ID shows her own number.

MAYA

Hello?

VOICE (ON PHONE)

Don't leave the house tomorrow.

Maya goes still. The voice is hers.

MAYA

Who is this?

That's the basic idea: show what happens, then use dialogue when someone needs to speak.

Your first draft

Your first script doesn't have to be perfect.

A first draft is where you discover the story. You can fix slow scenes, improve dialogue, and fill in missing details later. The goal is to get the idea out of your head and onto the page.

1

Write the story in plain language before worrying about screenplay format.

2

Keep each scene focused on something that changes, matters, or reveals new information.

3

Read it from beginning to end. Notice where you get confused or lose interest, then revise.

Common questions about writing a script

Can I write a script if I've never written one before?

Yes. Start by describing your idea in plain language. Work out who the story follows, what problem they face, and how it might end. Learn formatting as you turn that story into scenes.

What's the difference between a story and a script?

A story explains what happens. A script presents that story in a form people can perform and film, using scene headings, action descriptions, and dialogue.

Do I need to format my script from the beginning?

Not necessarily. It can help to plan your story in ordinary language first. When you're ready to write scenes, a screenplay guide or script editor can help you format the pages.

What can I write a script about?

You can start with a fictional idea, a real experience, a character, a mystery, a comedy, or one scene you can clearly imagine. The next step is finding the problem or change that makes the idea into a story.
