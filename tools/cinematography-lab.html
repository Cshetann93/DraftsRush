02 / Point of view

Camera angles and what they communicate

Meaning depends on blocking, performance, lens choice and context — not the angle alone.

Eye-level shot

A direct, observational viewpoint, useful when you want dialogue to feel grounded or relatively neutral.

High-angle shot

Looks down on a subject and can suggest vulnerability, exposure or a wider view of the space.

Low-angle shot

Looks up at a subject and can add presence, power, threat or a sense of scale.

Dutch angle

Tilts the horizon to create unease, instability or a deliberately off-balance visual world.

Bird’s-eye / overhead

Looks down from above to reveal patterns, blocking, isolation or the arrangement of objects.

Worm’s-eye angle

Places the camera very low, often exaggerating height, architecture or a subject's presence.

03 / Movement

Camera movements: pan, tilt, dolly, tracking and more

Give each move a purpose: reveal information, follow action, change distance or guide attention.

Pan

Rotate horizontally from a fixed position to follow action or reveal something across the frame.

Tilt

Rotate vertically to reveal height, follow a subject or shift attention up or down.

Dolly / push-in / pull-out

Physically move closer or farther away to intensify attention or reveal context.

Tracking shot

Move alongside, behind or ahead of a subject to sustain action and connect the audience to the character's path.

Crane / jib

Move through an elevated arc to reveal scale, geography or a changing perspective.

Handheld / Steadicam-style

Handheld can feel immediate and unstable; stabilised movement can preserve fluid motion through space.

Whip pan

A fast pan creates directional blur for energy, transitions or sudden attention shifts.

Zoom

Change focal length without moving the camera; unlike a dolly, perspective does not shift in the same way.

Dolly zoom

Move the camera while zooming in the opposite direction to shift background scale around the subject.

04 / Attention

Focus, composition and cinematic techniques

Shallow depth of field

Separate a face or object from a busy background using a narrow plane of focus.

Deep focus

Keep foreground and background detail readable so the audience can explore multiple layers.

Rack focus

Shift focus between subjects or depth planes to redirect attention without cutting.

Silhouette

Expose for a brighter background so the subject becomes a shape rather than a face.

Negative space

Leave visual room around a subject to communicate isolation, anticipation or imbalance.

Leading lines & foreground

Use architecture or foreground objects to guide the eye and create depth.

06 / Quick answers

Cinematography & Camera Shot FAQs

What are the main types of camera shots?

Common shot sizes include extreme wide, wide, full, medium, medium close-up, close-up and extreme close-up. OTS, POV, two-shot, reverse and insert shots are also widely used.

What is the difference between a shot size and a camera angle?

Shot size describes how much of the subject appears in frame; camera angle describes the viewpoint, such as eye-level, high-angle, low-angle, overhead or Dutch angle.

What is the difference between a dolly shot and a zoom?

A dolly physically moves the camera through space and changes perspective. A zoom changes focal length while the camera stays in place.

What is an over-the-shoulder shot?

It includes part of one character in the foreground while framing another, commonly used to connect viewpoints in dialogue.

How do I choose the right shot for a scene?

Start with the story beat: establish the location, show what a character notices, reveal a reaction or change how the audience reads the space. Then choose framing, angle, movement and focus to support that purpose.
