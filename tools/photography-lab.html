03 / Field guide

Camera Settings Cheat Sheet: ISO, Aperture & Shutter Speed

A practical cheat sheet for what to prioritise, why it matters, and what you trade off.

ISO / Light sensitivity

Keep it low when you can.

Use ISO 100–200 in bright daylight for clean detail. Try 400–800 in shade or interiors. ISO 1600+ can help at night or indoors when you cannot use a slower shutter, but expect more visible noise.

Prioritise ISO when…

You need a fast shutter in dim light, or you want the cleanest image possible and have enough light.

Aperture / Depth of field

Choose how much is in focus.

Use f/1.4–f/2.8 for portraits and low light when a shallow focus plane works. Try f/5.6–f/8 for groups, street scenes, and general sharpness. Use f/8–f/16 for landscapes when deeper focus matters.

Prioritise aperture when…

You want background separation, need more depth of field, or need to balance available light.

Shutter / Time

Decide whether motion stays sharp.

Start around 1/500s or faster for running subjects; 1/1000s or faster for many sports. Try 1/125s for still subjects handheld, and 1/15–1/30s for intentional light trails with a stable tripod.

Prioritise shutter when…

Your subject is moving, you are handholding the camera, or you want deliberate motion blur.

A quick exposure rule

If the image is too dark, open the aperture (smaller f-number), use a slower shutter, or raise ISO. If it is too bright, do the reverse. Change shutter carefully if you need to freeze motion, and change aperture carefully if depth of field matters.

04 / Quick answers

Exposure Triangle & Camera Settings FAQs

What is the exposure triangle in photography?

The exposure triangle describes how aperture, shutter speed and ISO work together to control a photograph's exposure. Each setting also affects the image differently: aperture changes depth of field, shutter speed affects motion blur, and ISO affects visible image noise.

What camera settings should beginners use?

Start with ISO 100–200 in bright daylight, around f/5.6–f/8 for general scenes, and a shutter speed fast enough for your subject. For portraits, try a wider aperture such as f/1.8–f/2.8; for sports, start around 1/1000s or faster. Treat these as starting points and check your camera's exposure meter.

Which camera setting controls background blur?

Aperture is one of the main controls for background blur. A wider aperture, shown by a smaller f-number such as f/1.8 or f/2.8, creates a shallower depth of field. Lens focal length, subject distance and the distance between your subject and background also affect the result.

What shutter speed freezes movement?

A fast shutter speed such as 1/500s can freeze many everyday movements, while sports and very fast action may need 1/1000s or faster. The right speed depends on how quickly the subject moves and how much motion blur you want.

Does this photography simulator use a real photo?

No. The simulator uses an illustrative scene and simplified visual effects to explain exposure, blur and noise. It is a learning aid, not a RAW image processor or a precise prediction of a specific camera and lens.
