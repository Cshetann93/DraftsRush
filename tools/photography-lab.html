03 / Quick Reference

Exposure Triangle Cheat Sheet

A direct summary of technical trade-offs across sensor sensitivity, optical aperture, and shutter timing.

ISO / Light Sensitivity

Keep low whenever light allows.

Stick to ISO 100–200 in direct sunlight for pristine dynamic range and zero digital grain. Use 400–800 in overcast shade or interiors. Push to 1600+ only when handholding in dark environments.

Priority Rule:

Prioritize low ISO for pristine commercial detail; raise ISO only when shutter or aperture cannot be adjusted further.

Aperture / Depth of Field

Control focus separation.

Use f/1.4–f/2.8 for portraits where you want cinematic background separation. Use f/5.6–f/8 for architecture, product photography, and street work. Move up to f/8–f/16 for landscapes.

Priority Rule:

Prioritize aperture whenever storytelling demands subject isolation or full edge-to-edge frame sharpness.

Shutter / Time & Motion

Control temporal motion.

Use 1/500s–1/2000s for running children, athletes, or fast wildlife. Use 1/125s for calm handheld subjects. Drop to 1/15s or slower on a heavy tripod for intentional cinematic light trails.

Priority Rule:

Prioritize shutter speed whenever motion blur or handshake threatens critical frame sharpness.

Practical Exposure Rule

If your frame is too dark: open the aperture (smaller f-stop), slow down your shutter speed, or raise ISO. If the frame is overexposed: close down the aperture (larger f-stop), speed up your shutter, or lower ISO.

04 / Questions & Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the exposure triangle in photography?

The exposure triangle describes how ISO, aperture, and shutter speed work collectively to record photographic exposure. Beyond brightness, each controls a creative consequence: aperture governs depth of field, shutter speed dictates motion blur, and ISO affects digital noise.

What settings should beginner photographers start with?

Start at ISO 100 in bright daylight, an aperture of f/4 to f/5.6 for general clarity, and a shutter speed of 1/250s to avoid camera shake. Adjust aperture if you want more blur, and speed up the shutter if your subject is moving.

Which camera control produces background blur?

A wide lens aperture (small f-number like f/1.8 or f/2.8) generates shallow depth of field. Combining a wide aperture with a longer focal length (e.g. 85mm) and close subject distance maximizes optical background separation.
