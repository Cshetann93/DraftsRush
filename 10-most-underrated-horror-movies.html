10 Most Underrated Horror Movies You Should Watch Today

Mainstream studio horror is saturated with cheap loud jump-stingers, frantic digital monsters, and formulaic haunted basements. The true masterpieces of dread often slip under the radar because they rely on atmosphere, psychological ambiguity, and auditory negative space. Here are the 10 most underrated horror films ever made—broken down in bullet points with what makes their craft special and why you need to watch them today.

TL;DR: The Underrated Horror Playbook CURATOR OVERVIEW
  • • Atmospheric Dread Over Jump Scares: These films build tension through what you cannot see rather than loud orchestral shocks.
  • • Grounded Emotional Stakes: Terror is tethered to grief, terminal guilt, marital decay, or social isolation.
  • • Subtle Visual Clues: Scares occur in the quiet corners of the background frame, rewarding attentive viewers.
CURATED BY: DRAFTSRUSH STUDIO • GENRE: PSYCHOLOGICAL & ATMOSPHERIC DREAD • 16 MIN READ
ENTRY 01 Dir. Joel Anderson • 2008

Lake Mungo (2008)

Subgenre: Found Footage / Documentary Grief Horror
  • The Narrative Setup: Sixteen-year-old Alice Palmer drowns while swimming at a local dam in Ararat, Australia. After her body is recovered and buried, her grieving family experiences inexplicable occurrences, strange photographic distortions, and sounds in their home, leading them to hire a parapsychologist to uncover Alice's secret double life.
  • What Makes It Special: It completely abandons Hollywood mockumentary clichés. There are no frantic shaky-cam chases; instead, Anderson employs calm television interviews, grainy home video footage, and cell phone captures. Dread builds not from demonic entities, but from the haunting realization that we can never truly know the internal sorrow of the people we live with.
  • Why You Should Watch It: The film delivers one of the most chilling, realistic jumpscare reveals in cinema history—captured on a grainy mobile phone camera on the shore of Lake Mungo—while concluding as a profound, heartbreaking meditation on loneliness and mortality.
ENTRY 02 Dir. Kiyoshi Kurosawa • 1997

Cure (1997)

Subgenre: Japanese Psychological Noir / Hypnotic Horror
  • The Narrative Setup: A series of grotesque murders plagues Tokyo, where different ordinary citizens butcher victims and carve an identical "X" into their chests. None of the killers possess a motive or remember committing the crime. A troubled detective, Kenichi Takabe, pursues an amnesiac man named Mamiya who wanders the coast asking disarming questions.
  • What Makes It Special: Kiyoshi Kurosawa treats evil as an airborne contagion transmitted through gentle conversation, dripping water, and lighter flames. There are no sudden orchestral stingers; scenes play out in static wide shots, forcing the audience to sit in suffocating procedural silence.
  • Why You Should Watch It: It is the spiritual forerunner to David Fincher’s Mindhunter and Zodiac[cite: 13]. It dissects how societal repressions can be unlocked with a single whisper, leaving you questioning your own psychological stability.
ENTRY 03 Dir. Brad Anderson • 2001

Session 9 (2001)

Subgenre: Architectural Claustrophobia / Psychological Unraveling
  • The Narrative Setup: An asbestos abatement crew wins a rushed, lucrative contract to clean out the massive, decaying Danvers State Mental Hospital in Massachusetts. As financial stress, exhaustion, and marital pressures rise among the men, one worker discovers nine archived audio reel tapes of a patient diagnosed with multiple personalities.
  • What Makes It Special: It was filmed on location inside the actual abandoned Danvers State Hospital before its demolition. The rotting peeling paint, damp tunnels, and institutional rot are completely genuine. Anderson leverages audio tapes as a parallel narrative track that slowly syncs up with the crew's deteriorating sanity.
  • Why You Should Watch It: It demonstrates how architectural space can become an active antagonist. The audio delivery of the final tape line ("I live in the weak and the wounded, Doc") provides pure, unadulterated auditory terror.
ENTRY 04 Dir. Karyn Kusama • 2015

The Invitation (2015)

Subgenre: Domestic Paranoia / Social Etiquette Suspense
  • The Narrative Setup: Will (Logan Marshall-Green) attends a dinner party at his former Hollywood Hills home hosted by his ex-wife Eden and her new husband David. As the night unfolds, strange guests, barred windows, locked doors, and an eerie cult video make Will suspect his hosts harbor sinister intentions for everyone in attendance.
  • What Makes It Special: It weaponizes polite social etiquette. Because neither Will nor the audience wants to appear rude or unhinged due to past grief, everyone ignores obvious warning signs. The tension comes from second-guessing whether Will is suffering a paranoid breakdown or if a mass murder is being prepared right in front of him.
  • Why You Should Watch It: It is a masterclass in slow-burn pacing inside a single contained location. The final five minutes widen the lens to reveal a haunting visual scope that will leave you stunned.

ENTRY 05 Dir. James Ward Byrkit • 2013

Coherence (2013)

Subgenre: Sci-Fi Psychological Horror / Micro-Budget Mystery
  • The Narrative Setup: Eight friends gather for a casual dinner party on the night an anomalous comet passes overhead. When the power cuts out and cell phones shatter, two guests venture into the dark neighborhood and discover an identical house down the street with eight identical people having the same conversation.
  • What Makes It Special: Byrkit shot the film in his own living room over five nights without a traditional script. Actors were handed daily index cards containing only their character's secret motives, forcing genuine confusion and organic, improvised tension as alternate realities bleed together.
  • Why You Should Watch It: It proves that mind-bending existential horror doesn’t require million-dollar visual effects—just a glow stick, a box of random household items, and an airtight mathematical concept.
ENTRY 06 Dir. Osgood Perkins • 2015

The Blackcoat's Daughter (2015)

Subgenre: Occult Slow-Burn / Nonlinear Grief Horror
  • The Narrative Setup: During winter break at an isolated Catholic boarding school, two students—Rose and the withdrawn freshman Kat (Kiernan Shipka)—are left behind after their parents mysteriously fail to pick them up. Simultaneously, a troubled young runaway named Joan hitches rides across the snowy landscape toward the school.
  • What Makes It Special: Osgood Perkins directs with cold, clinical stillness. The horror does not come from loud demonic outbursts, but from eerie mechanical noises in the basement furnace, unnatural physical contortions, and an oppressive winter color palette that suffocates hope.
  • Why You Should Watch It: It subverts the traditional possession formula by treating demonic possession not as an assault, but as a cure for crippling loneliness—concluding with a tragic, unforgettable emotional whimper.
ENTRY 07 Dir. Ben Wheatley • 2011

Kill List (2011)

Subgenre: British Hitman Noir / Folk Occult Horror
  • The Narrative Setup: Jay, an ex-soldier suffering from severe post-traumatic stress after a botched mission in Kyiv, struggles with marital discord and financial ruin. Pushed by his former military partner Gal, he accepts a contract hit list of three individuals, descending into a brutal rabbit hole of pagan rituals.
  • What Makes It Special: It pulls off a seamless mid-movie genre mutation: beginning as a gritty Ken Loach-style kitchen-sink domestic drama before mutating into a ruthless crime thriller, and finally collapsing into a nightmarish, pagan folk horror descent.
  • Why You Should Watch It: The final twenty minutes feature an overwhelming crescendo of tunnel claustrophobia, culminating in one of the most horrifying, gut-wrenching climactic confrontations in British cinema.
ENTRY 08 Dir. Natalie Erika James • 2020

Relic (2020)

Subgenre: Generational Decay / Metaphorical Haunted House
  • The Narrative Setup: When elderly matriarch Edna goes missing from her Victorian home, her daughter Kay and granddaughter Sam arrive to search for her. Edna mysteriously reappears with no memory of where she was, displaying erratic behavior and a growing black rot beneath her clothes as the walls of the house begin to physically warp.
  • What Makes It Special: It transforms the real-world terror of Alzheimer’s disease and dementia into physical architecture. The labyrinthine hallways behind the walls contract and shift like a deteriorating brain, making the psychological loss of identity tactile.
  • Why You Should Watch It: The final five minutes deliver a profoundly moving, tender, yet terrifying depiction of familial acceptance that elevates it far above standard haunted house tropes.
ENTRY 09 Dir. Matthew Holness • 2018

Possum (2018)

Subgenre: Psychological Surrealism / Childhood Trauma
  • The Narrative Setup: A disgraced, deeply traumatized puppeteer named Philip returns to his dilapidated childhood home in Norfolk, carrying a hideous leather bag containing his hideous creation: a giant spider-like puppet with a human face modeled on his own. As local children go missing, Philip tries repeatedly to destroy the puppet, but it always returns.
  • What Makes It Special: Written and directed by Matthew Holness (creator of Garth Marenghi's Darkplace), it is an uncompromisingly grim, somber study of childhood abuse. The Radiophonic sound design and desolate marsh landscapes evoke an inescapable, lingering nightmare.
  • Why You Should Watch It: Sean Harris delivers an agonizing, masterclass physical performance of an emotionally destroyed man, while the grotesque puppet design will haunt your dreams for weeks.
ENTRY 10 Dir. Remi Weekes • 2020

His House (2020)

Subgenre: Supernatural Refugee Horror / Survivor's Guilt
  • The Narrative Setup: A young refugee couple, Bol and Rial, escape war-torn South Sudan and make a perilous English Channel crossing, losing their daughter in the process. Granted temporary asylum, they are assigned a dilapidated public housing unit in London—only to discover that an ancient witch (an apeth) followed them across the ocean and lives inside the peeling walls.
  • What Makes It Special: It weaves the real-world horrors of immigration bureaucracy, xenophobia, and displacement directly into folklore. The couple cannot flee the house because reporting strange events risks immediate deportation, turning the building into an inescapable trap.
  • Why You Should Watch It: It features powerhouse performances from Sope Dirisu and Wunmi Mosaku, offering a heartbreaking third-act revelation about the unthinkable compromises human beings make simply to survive.
Horror Craft FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions: Underrated Horror

Why are these horror movies overlooked by casual viewers?

Most casual horror audiences anticipate fast pacing, recognizable monsters, and immediate jump scares. Slow-burn films prioritize atmospheric dread, psychological subtext, and patient negative space, meaning they build strong critical followings over time rather than instant box-office surges.

Which of these is best for fans of psychological thrillers?

Cure, Session 9, and The Invitation are top-tier recommendations. Each film relies entirely on character interaction, acoustic dread, and psychological breakdowns rather than supernatural creature attacks.
