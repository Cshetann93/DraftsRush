Parts of a Story: Freytag's Pyramid & Dramatic Structure

Every memorable film, video narrative, and script relies on an underlying scaffold. Dissect the five core parts of a story through Gustav Freytag's dramatic pyramid, examine real film examples, and learn how to keep audiences glued from page one to the final cut.

BY DRAFTSRUSH STUDIO • UPDATED FOR 2026 • 10 MIN READ
TL;DR // Quick Answer

Freytag's Pyramid Explained

Freytag's Pyramid is a 5-part dramatic structure model that maps narrative tension across an emotional mountain: starting with exposition, escalating through rising action, peaking at a central irreversible climax, descending through falling action, and settling into final resolution.

  • 1. Exposition: Establishes the protagonist, ordinary world, and rules of reality before disruption.
  • Inciting Incident: The catalyst that breaks the baseline routine and launches the journey.
  • 2. Rising Action: Obstacles compound, easy solutions fail, and stakes escalate progressively.
  • 3. Climax: The apex of peak tension demanding an irreversible physical or moral decision.
  • 4. Falling Action: The conflict unravels as characters face immediate consequences and fallout.
  • 5. Resolution (Dénouement): A new equilibrium is established, revealing how the journey changed the protagonist.

When a script feels boring, the problem is rarely the dialogue. It is almost always a failure of dramatic architecture. Writers often assemble scenes like a shopping list: first this happens, then this happens, then that happens. That is not storytelling; that is an itinerary.

In 1863, German novelist and playwright Gustav Freytag wrote Die Technik des Dramas. Analyzing classical Greek tragedies and Shakespearean five-act plays, Freytag noticed that all engaging drama mirrors a physiological heartbeat: tension rises slowly toward an apex of extreme pressure, snaps at an irreversible choice, and descends into consequence.

This triangular trajectory became known as Freytag's Pyramid. While modern cinema often operates on three-act or eight-sequence models, Freytag's five stages remain the raw foundation behind every high-retention video essay, indie short, and Hollywood blockbuster.

Part 01

Exposition (The Baseline)

ORDINARY WORLD

Exposition grounds the audience. It answers critical questions before the plot takes off: Who are we following? Where are we? What are the physical and emotional rules of this world? Most importantly, what internal flaw or tangible deficiency plagues the protagonist?

Amateur writers deliver exposition through clumsy dialogue where characters explain facts they already know. Experienced screenwriters convey exposition purely through behavior, contrast, and visual actions.

Case Study: A Quiet Place (2018)
Execution: The Abbott family scavenges a silent, ransacked store barefoot over lines of sand. Zero dialogue. The survival rule is established instantly: make noise, and die.
The Narrative Spark

The Inciting Incident: Destroying the Balance

Wedged directly between Exposition and Rising Action sits the Inciting Incident. Without this shockwave, your characters would stay locked in their daily routines forever.

A phone call delivers terrifying news, a letter arrives, an enemy breaches security, or a ticking deadline is declared. The inciting incident disrupts the protagonist's ordinary existence, forcing them out of comfort and demanding an active response.

Part 02

Rising Action (Compounding Conflict)

CAUSE & EFFECT ENGINE

The rising action represents the longest stretch of your script or video essay. It is not just a sequence of random fights or chases; it is an unforgiving chain of cause and effect.

Every time the protagonist attempts an easy solution, their shortcut must backfire. Each failure raises the physical, financial, or emotional stakes. If the character had three days to solve the mystery in scene two, by the midpoint they have sixty minutes, their trusted ally has fled, and the authorities suspect them of the crime.

Case Study: Jurassic Park (1993)
Execution: The tropical storm hits. The power grid drops. The fences go cold. The T-Rex breaks loose. Escape routes are cut off one by one, continuously narrowing survival options.

Part 03

The Climax (The Point of No Return)

THE APEX BEAT

The climax is the peak of Freytag's Pyramid. It is the moment where the central conflict reaches maximum tension and demands an immediate resolution.

Crucially, a real climax requires an irreversible choice. The protagonist can no longer hide behind compromise or avoidance. They must confront their central flaw or face the antagonist head-on. Whatever decision they make permanently changes their existence; returning to the old normal established in the exposition is impossible.

Case Study: The Dark Knight (2008)
Execution: Batman neutralizes the Joker, only to discover Harvey Dent's corruption. Batman chooses to take the blame for Dent's crimes, sacrificing his heroic status to preserve the city's hope.
Part 04

Falling Action (The Consequence Phase)

THE FALLOUT

Once the climatic peak resolves, the narrative enters the falling action. In Freytag's original tragic framework, this is where the protagonist's fortunes unravel toward disaster. In modern screenwriting, it acts as the fallout phase.

Secondary subplots conclude, ticking timers expire, and characters confront the physical and emotional scars left behind by the climax. Tension eases from active suspense into reflection and consequence.

Case Study: Whiplash (2014)
Execution: After walking off stage, Andrew returns to the drum kit for the definitive solo. The falling action is compressed into the final furious cues and the silent nod of validation from Fletcher before the cut to black.
Part 05

Resolution (The New Normal)

DÉNOUEMENT

The French term dénouement translates to "untying the knot." This is the final resting point of your narrative arc.

Show the new world order. How has the protagonist changed? In a tragedy, this is where surviving characters count the cost of unchecked hubris. In an uplifting arc, it shows the protagonist standing in their old arena with transformed moral clarity, carrying lessons bought through sacrifice.

Case Study: The Lord of the Rings: The Return of the King (2003)
Execution: The four hobbits sit in their local tavern back in the Shire. The surrounding patrons celebrate as normal, but the hobbits sit in silence. They are home, but their innocence is gone forever.

The 4-Point Narrative Pressure Audit

Before locking your script or recording your video concept, stress-test your outline against these structural benchmarks:

AUDIT 01
Is the inciting incident early enough? If your protagonist is still wandering through their daily routine on page fifteen or past the two-minute mark of a video essay, your hook is dead. Trigger disruption fast.
AUDIT 02
Do complications escalate consequences? If an obstacle merely delays the character without compounding the danger, delete it. Every rising action beat must increase the cost of ultimate failure.
AUDIT 03
Does the climax require an irreversible moral choice? A shootout or explosion is not a true climax if the character does not have to sacrifice something vital. Emotional stakes must drive physical resolution.
AUDIT 04
Is the ending earned by active character choices? If luck, convenient coincidences, or outside rescue save the protagonist in the final five minutes, the audience feels cheated. The outcome must stem from the protagonist's direct actions.
